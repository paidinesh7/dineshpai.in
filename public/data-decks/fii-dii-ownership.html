Why FII Selling Crashed the Market in 2008, But Not After 2022

Comparing the 2008 Global Financial Crisis with the 2022–2026 foreign sell-offs
2008 · WHEN FIIs SET THE PRICE
FIIs Sold ₹1.02 Lakh Cr
Nifty Fell 52%
Tiny Mutual Fund Base (3.1%)
In 2008, domestic mutual funds owned just 3.1% of the stock market. There was no steady monthly SIP book.
No Domestic Buyer Left
When foreign banks pulled out ₹1.02 lakh crore during the US crisis, domestic savers also pulled money out of mutual funds. Stock prices had to fall by half to find buyers.
2008 FII-to-DII Ownership Ratio: 1.55x
2024 TO 2025 · THE SIP COUNTERWEIGHT
FIIs Sold ₹6.10 Lakh Cr
DIIs Bought ₹13.15 Lakh Cr
₹32,000+ Cr Arrives Every Month via SIPs
Over 10 crore SIP accounts now send ~₹3.8 lakh crore a year into mutual funds, alongside steady inflows from EPFO, NPS, and insurance companies.
6x More Selling, Zero Crash
Even though FIIs sold 6 times more stock in 2024–2025 than they did in 2008, domestic institutions bought more than twice what FIIs sold.
2026 FII-to-DII Ownership Ratio: 0.84x

Three Things Worth Knowing About This Shift

What the headline FII and DII numbers do not show at first glance
01 · PRIMARY VS SECONDARY
FIIs Are Selling on the Exchange, Buying IPOs

Daily FII numbers only show stock exchange trades. While FIIs sold over ₹3 lakh crore a year on the exchange in 2024 and 2025, they put over ₹1.2 lakh crore a year into Indian IPOs and QIPs where they get large blocks at fixed prices.

02 · HOUSEHOLD OWNERSHIP
Indian Households Now Own 21.4% of the Market

Direct retail stock ownership looks unchanged since 2008 at ~9.9%. That is because millions of savers shifted from buying stocks directly to buying via Mutual Funds (11.5%). Combined, Indian households now own more than FIIs (16.1%).

03 · STEADY SIP BUYING
Domestic Funds Have to Keep Buying

Because ₹32,000+ crore comes in through SIPs every month and equity funds cannot hold huge cash piles, DIIs have to keep buying even when stocks are expensive. Promoters and FIIs have used this steady demand to cash out at high prices.
