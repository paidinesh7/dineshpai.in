Notes on the Next Ten Years

Before getting into specific sectors, I want to share how I look at businesses and why some of them become good investment opportunities. That lens shapes where we are placing bets in India over the next decade.

01 / First Principles

Businesses • Supply & demand • Capital

Why does a business exist?

At its core, a business solves a problem that a customer is willing to pay for, and does it at a cost lower than what the customer pays. Fundraising, valuations, and brand campaigns all come later.

Whether a business stays profitable over time comes down to supply and demand. If ten other people can set up the same supply in six months, competition will eat your margins. We see this all the time with thin software tools and consumer brands that use the same contract manufacturers. Everyone ends up spending heavily on marketing just to stay visible.

Good businesses sit where demand is growing, but adding new supply is hard. You rarely find these gaps in macro reports. You find them by picking a specific niche and going deep into how the product is made, why customers are unhappy with current options, and what stops a competitor from copying it quickly.

What makes it an investment opportunity?

A good business is not always a good venture investment. Many good businesses do not need outside equity at all. They can grow comfortably from their own customer cash flows.

An investment makes sense when adding capital unlocks an outcome that would not happen otherwise. Maybe a founder needs capital to set up a factory, spend three years on R&D before revenue starts, or build a rural supply chain.

At Rainmatter, our capital comes from Zerodha's balance sheet. We have no external LPs and no ten-year fund cycle. We do not need quick markups. We can look at a niche, figure out what kind of time and capital the problem needs, and wait while the team builds.

02 / Household Incomes

PRICE ICE 360° Survey • 2020-21 vs. 2030-31E

When we look at India over the next decade, the biggest shift is in household incomes.

In 2020-21, India had about 301 million households. Roughly two-thirds earned less than ₹5 lakh a year. By 2030-31, the bottom two brackets will shrink in absolute terms. Around 74 million households will move into the middle class (₹5 to ₹30 lakh), and another 24 million will cross ₹30 lakh.

03 / Tax Filings

CBDT & e-Filing Portal • FY21 vs. FY26

Survey projections can be off, so I like checking them against income tax filings.

Between FY21 and FY26, individual tax returns grew from 6.37 crore to 8.22 crore. Almost all of this growth came from higher brackets. Filings under ₹5 lakh dropped by 20%, while the ₹5 to ₹10 lakh bracket grew 81%. People declaring between ₹10 lakh and ₹50 lakh more than doubled to 1.50 crore, and filings above ₹1 crore grew 128%.

04 / Consumer Scarcity

Rising incomes • Tight physical supply

Once a family crosses ₹10 lakh in annual income, basic needs like food, clothes, and mobile data are sorted. Extra income goes toward a better home, travel, health, clean food, and managing savings.

When millions of families want these things at the same time, physical supply struggles to keep up. You cannot build a good hotel, a clean dairy supply chain, or a hospital overnight. Japan in the 1970s, South Korea in the late 1980s, and China after 2008 all went through this same phase after crossing $3,000 in per-capita GDP.

Five areas where I see demand running ahead of supply:

  • Housing: Families moving up from basic apartments want well-run communities with reliable water, power, and open spaces. Good urban land with clean titles and credible builders is hard to find.
  • Travel and Leisure: We have built airports and highways faster than good places to stay. India still lacks clean, mid-priced hotels and holiday spots within driving distance of big cities.
  • Clean Food: Rising incomes have come alongside high rates of diabetes and metabolic illness. More families now read labels and want clean staples. The hard part here is not branding or 10-minute delivery. It is farm sourcing, soil health, and cold chains (Akshayakalpa, The Whole Truth, Two Brothers).
  • Financial Services: As more people move savings from gold and fixed deposits into financial markets, they need simple tools and honest advice to manage their money (Capitalmind, Assetplus, Mprofit).
  • Cooling and Healthcare Hardware: Less than 10% of Indian homes have an AC today, compared to over 60% in China. As summers get hotter and incomes rise, demand for efficient cooling, motors, and airflow hardware (Karban) will keep growing. On healthcare, as more parents age in Tier-2 and Tier-3 towns, we need affordable diagnostics and out-of-hospital recovery care (Sukino).

05 / What India Imports Today

Ministry of Mines • CSEP • GTRI • IMTMA

Rising incomes tell you what Indian households will buy more of. Trade data tells you where our factories and infrastructure are still fragile.

Over the last decade, India has set up assembly lines for phones, EVs, solar panels, and medicines. That is real progress. Still, if you look one layer deeper at the metals, chemicals, magnets, and machines that feed those factories, we import almost everything from one or two countries.

For years, buying from the cheapest global supplier worked fine. Today, countries use export controls on minerals, magnets, and factory tools as leverage. If access is tightened tomorrow, several large sectors in India will struggle to keep running.

06 / Industrial & Sovereign Tech

Power grid • Factory machines • Metals

That import table is our roadmap for industrial investing. A country of 1.4 billion people cannot rely entirely on imports for its power grid, factory machines, critical materials, or defense.

You cannot fix these gaps with a software wrapper or a quick trading business. Solving them takes five to eight years of metallurgy, hardware engineering, and factory testing before large revenues show up. That is why patient capital fits naturally here.

  • The Power Grid: Setting up solar plants is no longer the main problem. Moving and storing that power is. As EVs, ACs, factories, and data centers grow, we need large battery storage, transformers, fire-safety materials (Voltseal), and commercial clean energy (Neufin, Solarsquare).
  • Engineering Around Missing Materials: When you do not have local deposits of rare earths or lithium, you have two choices. You can build refining and recycling capacity at home, or you can engineer products that do not need the imported material at all. That is why we backed Naxatra, which designs high-efficiency motors without rare-earth magnets.
  • Factory Machines: Walk through an Indian factory making electronics or auto parts today, and almost all the CNC machines, spindles, and testing rigs are imported. Building precision machines and factory tools in India is a multi-decade opportunity.
  • Space and Defense: In strategic sectors, imported hardware can be cut off when you need it most. We back Indian teams building their own satellites (Galaxeye) and rocket propulsion (Spacefields).
