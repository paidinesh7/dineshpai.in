First Principles & Ten-Year Outlook 2026 — 2036

How I think about businesses, investing, and the next ten years in India.

Before talking about specific sectors or 10-year projections, I want to lay out how I think about businesses from the ground up—why businesses exist, how supply and demand shape returns, what makes a company an actual investment opportunity, and where that lens points me in India over the next decade.

01 / First Principles Businesses, Niches & Matching Capital to Impact

What is a business, and why does it exist?

Stripped of all the startup jargon, a business exists for one basic reason: to solve a problem that a customer is willing to pay for, at a cost lower than what they pay you. Everything else—fundraising, team size, brand campaigns, valuations—is secondary to that basic exchange.

Once a business is up and running, its durability comes down to supply and demand. If you are selling something where ten other competitors can set up supply in six months—which is true for most thin software apps or marketing-led consumer brands using the same contract manufacturers—competition quickly eats away your margins. You end up spending all your energy and capital fighting for customer attention. On the other hand, when demand for a product or service is growing steadily while new supply is genuinely hard to add, a business gets the breathing room to earn healthy margins and compound over time.

Broad macro narratives rarely tell you where those supply-demand imbalances sit. To find them, you have to pick one specific niche and go deep into the details—understanding why existing options fall short for the customer, how the underlying supply chain or technology actually works, and what stops a new entrant from copying it overnight.

How to think about an investment opportunity

A good business and a good investment opportunity are not always the same thing. Plenty of great businesses do not need outside equity at all; they can and should grow steadily out of their own customer cash flows.

An investment opportunity exists when matching capital to a specific business helps unlock real impact that would not happen otherwise. That impact can take different forms: helping a founder build factory capacity or supply-chain infrastructure, funding multi-year engineering and R&D before revenue kicks in, creating rural livelihoods, or helping Indians make better health, nutrition, and financial decisions.

At Rainmatter, because we invest from a permanent balance sheet with no external LPs and no fixed fund deadlines, we do not have to force every company into a 3-year markup cycle. We can look at a niche, ask what kind of capital and time horizon the problem actually needs, and stay patient while the team builds supply.

02 / Household Income Graduation Where New Demand Comes From (2021 → 2031E)

When I apply that supply-and-demand lens to India over the next ten years (2026–2036), the starting point is understanding how household purchasing power is changing across income slabs.

According to the ICE 360° household surveys by PRICE (People Research on India's Consumer Economy), India had roughly 301 million households in 2020–21. At the start of this decade, two-thirds of the population lived in the lowest two brackets—earning under ₹5 lakh a year per household. Over the ten years to 2030–31, the bottom two slabs shrink in absolute numbers while 74 million additional households graduate into the middle class (₹5–30 lakh) and 24 million additional households enter the affluent bracket (>₹30 lakh).

03 / What Income Tax Filings Show 2021 vs. 2025–26 Slab Migration

Survey projections can sometimes feel abstract, so it is useful to check them against actual tax filings from the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) and the Income Tax e-Filing portal.

Between Assessment Year 2021–22 (FY21) and FY 2025–26, the total number of individual tax returns grew by 29%, from 6.37 crore to 8.22 crore. What matters is where that growth happened. Returns filed in the lowest slab (up to ₹5 lakh) fell by 20% as earners moved into higher brackets and tax-rebate thresholds rose. Meanwhile, filers in the ₹5–10 lakh slab rose 81%, filers earning ₹10–50 lakh more than doubled from 72 lakh to 1.50 crore, and individuals declaring over ₹1 crore grew 128%.

04 / Theme 1: Consumer Scarcity Where Disposable Income Meets Tight Supply

When tens of millions of families cross ₹10 lakh in household income, basic necessities are already taken care of. Incremental income goes into upgrading how they live: better homes, holidays, dining and entertainment, preventive health, financial products, and household hardware.

In software, if demand doubles, a company can spin up more cloud servers in an afternoon. In the physical world, when 50 million families simultaneously want better housing, a weekend holiday, clean dairy, or air conditioning, supply cannot adjust quickly. We saw this exact pattern play out in Japan in the 1970s, South Korea in the late 1980s, and China after 2008.

Over the next decade, I am most interested in five specific niches where rising middle-class spending runs into genuine physical or trust bottlenecks:

  • Housing & Well-Run Communities: As nuclear families upgrade from basic apartments, the shortage is not in luxury skyscrapers—it is in thoughtfully designed, energy- and water-resilient communities with reliable execution. Developable urban land with clean titles and credible developers remains scarce.
  • Travel, Hospitality & Leisure Experiences: Airport capacity, Vande Bharat trains, and expressways have expanded much faster than good places to stay. Outside a handful of luxury chains, India has a massive shortage of clean, reliable mid-market hotels, homestays, and leisure destinations within a 3–4 hour drive of major cities.
  • Clean, Traceable Food & Nutrition: Higher incomes in India have unfortunately come alongside rising diabetes and metabolic disease. Families are actively looking for chemical-free staples, high-protein foods, and clean labels. The bottleneck here is not branding or quick-commerce delivery; it is farm-level sourcing, soil health, and cold-chain logistics (Akshayakalpa, The Whole Truth, Two Brothers).
  • Transparent Financial Services: As tens of millions of tax-filing earners move savings out of physical gold and bank deposits into mutual funds, equities, and insurance, they need unconflicted advice and software infrastructure to manage their money (Capitalmind, Assetplus, Mprofit).
  • Scarce Consumer & Medical Hardware (Cooling & Diagnostics): Only ~9% of Indian households own an air conditioner today, compared to over 60% in China. As incomes rise in a warming climate, India will add over 100 million room ACs, commercial chillers, and agricultural cold-chains—creating strong demand for efficient BLDC motors, compressors, and thermal materials. On the healthcare side, as 150 million Indians cross age 60, we will need affordable point-of-care diagnostic devices and out-of-hospital rehabilitation centers across Tier-2 and Tier-3 towns.
05 / Theme 2: Sovereign Industrial Stack Grid Infrastructure, Machinery & Metals

My second theme is sovereign technologies and industrial resilience. Over the last few years, export controls and supply-chain disruptions have made one thing obvious: a country of 1.4 billion people cannot rely on imports for its power grid, factory machinery, critical metals, communications, and defense.

Building domestic capability in these areas is not a short-term policy fad; it is something successive governments will have to support for the next twenty years. While space and defense get most of the headlines, I am equally interested in the industrial niches underneath them—grid infrastructure, industrial machinery, and specialty metals.

  • Grid Infrastructure & Power Electronics: Generating solar power is no longer the bottleneck in India; evacuating and storing it is. As electric vehicles, air conditioners, factories, and data centers come online, our transmission and distribution grid needs massive upgrades. I am keen on companies building grid-scale battery storage, transformer components, thermal fire-safety materials (Voltseal), and commercial clean-energy infrastructure (Neufin, Solarsquare).
  • Industrial Machinery ("The Machines That Make the Machines"): Walk through almost any Indian factory assembling electronics, auto parts, or aerospace components today, and the high-precision CNC machines, spindles, and automated testing rigs are still imported from Germany, Japan, Taiwan, or China. As Indian manufacturing scales, localizing precision machine tools, industrial sensors, and factory automation is a massive multi-decade opportunity.
  • Specialty Metals & Material Science: If you look one layer below India's transformer shortage, nearly 90% of Cold-Rolled Grain-Oriented (CRGO) electrical steel still has to be imported. Electric motors face a similar choke point with imported rare-earth magnets. I want to back founders who either engineer around imported materials—like Naxatra building high-efficiency axial-flux motors without rare-earth magnets—or build domestic refining, metallurgy, and critical mineral recycling.
  • Satellites, Communications & Defense: In strategic sectors, buying off-the-shelf from abroad always carries the risk of supply cutoffs when you need the hardware most. Backing Indian engineering teams building indigenous multi-sensor satellites (Galaxeye) and rocket propulsion (Spacefields) fits squarely into this view.
