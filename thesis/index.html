01 / First Principles

Businesses • Supply & demand • Capital

Why does a business exist?

A business exists to solve a problem a customer will pay for, at a cost lower than what they pay. Fundraising, valuations, and brand campaigns come later.

Whether it stays profitable comes down to supply and demand. If ten others can set up the same supply in six months, competition will eat your margins. You see this with thin software tools and consumer brands using the same contract manufacturers. Everyone ends up spending heavily on marketing just to stay visible.

Good businesses sit where demand is growing, but new supply takes years to build. You rarely find these gaps in macro reports. You find them by going deep into a niche: how the product is made, why current options fall short, and what stops someone from copying it quickly.

What makes it an investment?

A good business is not always a good venture investment. Many good businesses can grow comfortably from their own customer cash flows. Outside capital only makes sense when it unlocks something that would not happen otherwise.

  • Paid for without marketing dollars: Customers buy because the product solves a real problem, not for discounts.
  • Supply is the moat: Whether it is village milk collection, soil testing, or five-axis machining, the hard part sits in physical execution rather than ad spend.
  • Upfront effort turns into operating leverage: Building a factory, hospital network, or hardware product is slow early on. Once capacity fills up, unit costs fall and cash flows compound.
  • Inelastic pricing power: Buyers care more about reliability, purity, or uptime than saving 5% on price. That leaves room for healthy margins without price wars.
  • Capital that matches duration: If an engineering or supply chain problem takes six to eight years to solve, you cannot build it with money that needs a quick markup in two years.

02 / Household Incomes

PRICE ICE 360° Survey • 2020-21 vs. 2030-31E

The biggest shift in India over the next decade is in household incomes.

In 2020-21, two-thirds of India's 301 million households earned less than ₹5 lakh a year. By 2030-31, the bottom two brackets will shrink in absolute numbers. Around 74 million households will move into the middle class (₹5 to ₹30 lakh), and another 24 million will cross ₹30 lakh.

03 / Tax Filings

CBDT & e-Filing Portal • FY21 vs. FY26

Survey projections can be off, so it helps to check them against actual income tax returns.

Between FY21 and FY26, individual tax filings grew from 6.37 crore to 8.22 crore, driven by higher brackets. Filings under ₹5 lakh dropped 20%, while the ₹5 to ₹10 lakh bracket grew 81%. Filers between ₹10 lakh and ₹50 lakh more than doubled to 1.50 crore, and those above ₹1 crore grew 128%.

About 1.62 crore Indians now declare over ₹10 lakh a year, double the number from five years ago. Still, that is just over 1% of our population. The base is growing fast, and it is still early.

04 / Consumer Scarcity

Rising incomes • Tight physical supply

Once a family crosses ₹10 lakh in annual income, basic needs like grains, clothes, and mobile data are covered. Extra income goes toward a better home, travel, health, clean food, and savings.

When millions of families want these things at once, physical supply struggles to keep up. You cannot build a good hotel, a clean dairy supply chain, or a hospital overnight. Japan in the 1970s, South Korea in the late 1980s, and China after 2008 went through this same shift after crossing $3,000 in per-capita GDP.

India enters this phase with very low physical capacity per person, so the runway to build locally is long.

Five niches where supply lags

In each of these areas, demand is growing fast while new supply takes years of land, trust, or execution to add.

  • Housing: Families upgrading from basic apartments want reliable water, power, and open spaces. Good city land with clean titles and trustworthy builders takes years to put together.
  • Travel and Leisure: We have built highways and airports faster than good places to stay. India has about 1.8 lakh branded hotel rooms across the whole country, less than many single cities in Asia. Clean, mid-priced stays near big cities are hard to find.
  • Clean Food: With rising diabetes and lifestyle illness, more families read labels and want clean staples. The hard part is not branding or 10-minute delivery. It is farm sourcing, soil testing, and cold chains that keep milk and produce fresh without preservatives.
  • Financial Services: As households move money from gold and fixed deposits into financial markets, they need simple products and honest advice that does not depend on commissions.
  • Healthcare: As parents age in nuclear families, hospitals alone cannot handle long-term care. We need affordable diagnostics, rehab centers, and home care that keep patients out of hospital beds.

05 / What India Imports Today

Ministry of Mines • CSEP • GTRI • IMTMA

Rising incomes show what households will buy. Trade data shows where our factories and infrastructure remain fragile.

Over the last decade, India has set up assembly lines for phones, EVs, solar panels, and medicines. Still, if you look one layer deeper at the metals, chemicals, magnets, and machines feeding those factories, we import almost everything from one or two countries.

Buying from the cheapest global supplier worked when trade was open. Today, countries use export controls on minerals, magnets, and factory tools as leverage. If supply tightens tomorrow, large parts of Indian industry will struggle to run.

Small input, big hold-up

Often, the imported part is a small fraction of the final product's cost, yet missing it stops the entire line. You cannot ship a ₹12 lakh electric car without ₹3,500 worth of magnets, and you cannot turn on a ₹500 crore solar park without a ₹4 crore transformer.

That cost gap makes buyers care far more about reliable supply than saving a few percent on price.

06 / Industrial & Sovereign Tech

Power grid • Factory machines • Metals

A country of 1.4 billion people cannot rely on imports for its power grid, factory machines, critical materials, and defense.

You cannot fix these gaps with a software tool or a quick trading business. Solving them takes five to eight years of metallurgy, hardware engineering, and factory testing before large revenues show up. That is why patient capital fits naturally here.

Four areas to build at home

Pure assembly works only when global supply chains stay cheap and calm. Domestic engineering works in calm times, and matters even more when trade tightens.

  • The Power Grid: Setting up solar plants is no longer the main bottleneck; moving and storing that power is. As EVs, factories, and data centers grow, India needs grid batteries, transformers, fire-safety materials, and simpler ways for factories to buy clean power.
  • Engineering Around Missing Materials: Where India lacks local deposits of rare earths or lithium, there are two options: build refining and recycling capacity at home, or design products that do not need the imported material—like motors built without rare-earth magnets.
  • Factory Machines: Walk through an Indian factory making electronics or auto parts today, and almost all the CNC machines, spindles, and testing rigs are imported. Building precision factory tools in India is a multi-decade opportunity.
  • Space and Defense: Imported hardware gets cut off right when you need it most. India needs domestic teams building satellites, radar sensors, and rocket propulsion from scratch.
