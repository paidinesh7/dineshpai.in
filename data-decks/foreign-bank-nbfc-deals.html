Why Are Foreign Investors Buying Into Indian Lenders?

Three practical reasons why global banks and funds are writing such large cheques right now
01 · BUYING VS BUILDING
Building From Scratch Takes Too Long

Foreign banks like Citi and HSBC tried running their own branches in India for decades and struggled to grow beyond a few big cities. Buying into IDBI or partnering with Jio or Shriram gives a foreign buyer thousands of branches, local staff, and millions of customers from day one.

02 · SLOW HOME MARKETS
Cheap Money Looking for 12–15% Growth

Japanese banks like MUFG and SMBC sit on huge piles of low-cost deposits in a home market that barely grows. UAE banks like Emirates NBD want to serve the millions of Indians and businesses moving money between the Gulf and India, where loan demand is growing 12–15% a year.

03 · CLEAN BALANCE SHEETS
The Old Bad Loan Problem Is Over

Five years ago, foreign investors stayed away because Indian banks were stuck with old corporate bad loans. Today, those books are clean. IDBI Bank, which had ~30% bad loans in 2019, is now down to 0.15% net bad loans and made ₹9,513 Cr in profit last year.

Why Does It Make Sense for the Indian Side?

What the government, mid-sized banks, and Indian NBFCs get out of these deals
01 · FOR LIC, SBI & THE GOVT
Getting Rescue Money Back at a Profit

When IDBI (2019) and Yes Bank (2020) were failing, LIC and SBI stepped in to save them. LIC put ₹26,367 Cr into IDBI. In this sale, LIC gets ~₹26,440 Cr back in cash for half its stake, and still keeps a 19% stake worth ~₹16,580 Cr. SBI similarly exited Yes Bank to SMBC at a profit.

02 · FOR NBFCs (JIO, SHRIRAM)
Fresh Capital and Lower Borrowing Costs

Unlike banks, NBFCs cannot take public savings deposits; they have to borrow money from the market to lend it out. Having Bank of America (in Jio Credit) or MUFG (in Shriram) put in ₹18,000–40,000 Cr gives them long-term money and helps them borrow at lower interest rates.

03 · FOR MID-SIZED BANKS
Money to Compete With the Top 4 Banks

India's four biggest private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, and Kotak) attract the lion's share of cheap deposits. Smaller banks like RBL, Yes Bank, IDFC FIRST, and Federal Bank need large, patient backers so they can open more branches and lend more aggressively.

Three Common Questions About These Deals

Why Indian corporate groups aren't buying banks, and how the RBI keeps control
QUESTION 01
Why Aren't Big Indian Groups Buying Banks?

The RBI does not allow corporate houses (like Tata, Reliance, Birla, or Adani) to own banks. The rule is simple: if a business group owns a bank, it might use public depositors' money to give easy loans to its own companies. Corporate groups can only run NBFCs, which don't take public deposits.

QUESTION 02
Why Aren't HDFC or ICICI Buying Them?

Big Indian banks already have branches in every major neighbourhood. Paying ₹50,000 Cr in cash for a mid-sized bank means paying for duplicate branches and taking on an older cost structure. For a foreign buyer entering India, that same branch network is brand new reach.

QUESTION 03
Does a 60% Stake Mean Full Foreign Control?

No. Even when Fairfax buys 60.72% of IDBI Bank or Emirates NBD buys 60% of RBL Bank, Indian banking law caps any single shareholder's voting rights at 26%. They get 60% of the economic profits, but voting power stays capped and the bank remains under tight RBI rules.

Source: Banking Regulation Act 1949 (Section 12) & RBI Ownership Guidelines 05 / 05
